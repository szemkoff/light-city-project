The city

A city laid out like a living system.

Concentric rings from a central spire to a wild buffer: gathering at the heart, homes in the middle, food and energy at the edge. Everything is walkable.

The rings

From the center outward.

The plan follows concentric circles joined by radial paths, like spokes. Quiet and shared life sits at the center; production and wild land sit at the edge.

  1. Center

    Spire & plaza

    The landmark tower, the main plaza, markets and festivals.

  2. Inner ring

    Temple & learning

    Temple, quiet rooms, library, learning center and clinic.

  3. Middle ring

    Studios & domes

    Maker studios, co-working and the communosphere domes with their apartments.

  4. Outer ring

    Homes & gardens

    Family ring housing, tiny-home clusters, greenhouses and food forest.

  5. Edge

    Energy & wild buffer

    Solar microgrid, water treatment, fields and a protected woodland margin.

Key structures

What gets built, and how ready it is.

Each structure shows its Technology Readiness Level (TRL): 9 means proven at scale, 1 means early research.

Central sanctum

The Crystal Point spire

TRL 4–5 at full scale

Shaped like a natural quartz point: twelve sides of faceted solar glass diamonds, blue and violet above and amber below, on a white steel diagrid, 61 m across at the foot and narrowing to a faceted glass point. Near the top the glass climbs on a slant into an open lattice where the wind turbines turn. It is the city's orientation point, seen from every path by day and glowing softly at night. Inside are a base hall, a gathering level, an enclosed observatory at 72 m, a water tank for the town, a learning level and a star room in the point.

In Phase 3 the top opens into a wind crown: six helical turbines, three per level, the upper row turned 60 degrees. Together with the solar glass they give very roughly 0.5–1 GWh a year, a supplement rather than the city's supply. The foundation is sized for 240 m from the start, so later phases add sections on top instead of rebuilding, as the Shukhov Tower in Moscow was built from stacked sections. See it in 3D.

Neighborhood

Communosphere

TRL 7–9

Each communosphere is a neighborhood under one dome: apartments terraced around a planted atrium, a shared kitchen and dining hall, and wellness spaces. The shell creates a mild indoor climate, cutting heating and cooling needs.

Geodesic domes and cross-laminated timber are both proven. The new part is combining them at this size with homes inside, which is a design challenge rather than a research one.

Inner ring

Temple & plaza

TRL 9

A non-denominational temple for silence, meditation and community ceremonies, proportioned with sacred geometry, with a single upright quartz point on a stone pedestal under the oculus and a shallow water basin at the center. Participation in any practice is always voluntary.

The plaza beside it is the city's living room: morning markets, performances, seasonal festivals and the everyday crossing point of the whole community.

Middle ring

Studios & workshops

TRL 9

Shared studios make it possible to live in a small home and still have access to a full woodshop, ceramics, textiles, digital fabrication and quiet co-working.

They also support the local economy: residents can teach workshops, sell what they make and repair what the city needs.

Outer ring

Greenhouses & food forest

TRL 8–9

Year-round solar greenhouses, aquaponics and a layered food forest supply a meaningful share of fresh produce in Phase 1, growing to 5–10 ha and near self-sufficiency in Phase 2. Compost from kitchens returns to the soil, closing the loop.

Phase 1 is deliberately modest: staple grains and some goods will still come from regional farms, which also builds ties with neighbors.

Edge

Microgrid

TRL 8–9

A community-owned solar microgrid with battery storage powers homes, workshops and greenhouses. It stays connected to the regional grid for resilience and can island during outages.

All of this is proven, off-the-shelf technology. The design work is in sizing, siting and making the energy flows visible to residents.

Growth by phase

Built in steps, each one proven before the next.

One radial plan grows outward from the same spire and plaza, and nothing built in one phase is torn down in the next. One 3D model shows all three: open it at any phase and switch phases to turn on what each step adds.

Phase 1 · 200 residents

Pilot hamlet

A 90 m spire, one temple and two learning pavilions, three 40 m domes and about 30 earth homes on a 20–30 acre core. Reserved dome plots hold meadow and a tree nursery. The Spire Park, a care farm, a swimming pond and the first woods are there from the start.

Open the model at Phase 1 →

Phase 2 · 1,500 residents

Village

Everything from Phase 1 stays. The spire gains two sections (150 m), five domes fill reserved plots, courtyard clusters of earth homes form a new ring and a food and energy belt is added. The Council House, a festival meadow, stables, a boathouse, a primary school and a health centre arrive. About 160 acres.

Open the model at Phase 2 →

Phase 3 · 8,000+ residents

Town

The village and its belt stay as built. The crystal spire reaches 240 m with its wind crown, and the last ring domes are filled; outside the green belt come flowing terraces of glass, timber and thin-shell concrete with green roofs, six 80 m district domes and a new outer belt, with an event hall, a sports park, a secondary school and a town health centre. About 1.2 square miles.

Beyond the box

Curved, healthy and affordable.

High-tech design, low-tech materials: computers find efficient curved shapes, then we build them from earth, stone, timber, bamboo and lime that residents can handle, chosen from what each site provides. Curves use less material, hold heat better and are rated more beautiful in brain-imaging studies.

Tiny homes

Earth homes from local soil

Proven to Emerging

Tiny-home clusters can be built from superadobe (earth-filled bags coiled into domes) or printed from raw earth, as in the TECLA prototype in Italy. Residents can help build, and that work can count toward contribution-based residency.

Earth walls breathe, store heat and contain no synthetic finishes. Durability in wet or freezing climates still needs proof, so we test before we commit.

Light on the land

Mostly wild land, measured every year.

Zero impact is not possible, but half a normal footprint is: ecovillages like Findhorn and Sieben Linden already live at about half their national averages. We build a compact core, run on our own sun and rain, protect the night and leave more nature than we found.

Land

Build small

A 20–30 acre core on a 100–500 acre site. At least half the land stays permanent habitat, with wildlife corridors kept open.
