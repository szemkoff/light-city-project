4 · Financial model and sensitivity

Where the model holds, and where it does not.

Rebuilt around households. Earlier versions counted every resident as paying a full home fee ($925 a month each). Fees are charged per home, so the model now counts homes: about 110 homes for 200 residents, plus a member fee for each extra person.

At full occupancy and mid prices, home fees bring in about $1.37M a year and member fees about $250K (about 50 extra adults and 40 children), so $1.62M in all. The earlier per-resident method gave $2.0M at 90% occupancy. Who lives in which home: who fits where.

  • Break-even: at mid prices, the operating budget falls short even when every home is full. Fees about 13% above mid prices (close to the top of each range) are needed to break even at 90% occupancy with base other income, and about 30% above mid with low retreat income.
  • Not yet included: discounts for contribution-based residency (20–30% of homes) and founding-member discounts. Both lower income further.
  • Ways to close the gap, to test in Stage 2: more homes on the same infrastructure, a higher share of 1-, 2- and 3-bedroom homes, a higher member fee, a lower operating cost per home, or retreat income secured by pre-sales.
  • Capex: the cost plan assumed 50–100 homes. About 110 homes, including 10 three-bedroom homes, may add about 10% to the housing line; this will be re-estimated in Stage 2. A +20% overrun on all construction takes it to about $54M and adds about $9M to the raise.
  • Economy plan: the inside economy moves step by step from dollar fees to Light hours, a time credit. The dial never goes below the edge bill (loan payments, taxes, insurance, outside costs and reserves), and it steps back automatically if that bill is not covered. See Economy and Migration.
  • Open item, debt service: the operating model does not yet include interest. If the EPC share (about $9M) costs 6–8%, interest is $540K–$720K a year. Stage 2 will rebuild the model with debt terms, reserves and scenario IRRs.

5 · Comparables

What other communities teach us.

Real projects, good and hard, and the lesson we take from each.

Findhorn (Scotland)

Spiritual community since 1962, roughly 300–500 people in the wider community. The Findhorn Foundation closed its educational programmes in September 2023.

Lesson: Retreat and education income can stop. We model a low-retreat case and keep it off the critical path.

Wikipedia · Moray Council report

Sieben Linden (Germany)

Ecovillage founded in 1997, around 140 residents, growing slowly toward a planned 300. About 20 years in, 11 houses stood on site.

Lesson: Community-built growth is slow. Phase 1 is professionally built; self-build is optional.

Transit Social Innovation · Global Ecovillage Network

Serenbe (Georgia, US)

Wellness-focused community of about 1,500 residents and 800 homes, with 70% of its land preserved. Its 2002 zoning overlay took years of work with the county.

Lesson: Zoning is a multi-year task. Permitting is a Stage 2 workstream, not an afterthought.

Serenbe

Babcock Ranch (Florida, US)

First residents in 2018. More than 15,000 residents and 5,516 homes sold by December 31, 2025. A 150 MW solar plant on 870 acres is owned by the utility, FPL.

Lesson: A utility partnership can carry the energy capex. We will test that route at each shortlisted site.

SWFL Business Today · Babcock Ranch media kit

Intended structure, to be confirmed with counsel.

7 · Exit and liquidity

A 7–12 year horizon.

Proposed mechanisms from the development stages documentation. Terms will be set in the offering documents.

    Preferred return

    A target 4–6% preferred return once operations begin.

  2. Years 5–7

    Refinance

    Refinancing with permanent debt aims to return investor capital in full.

  3. Year 10+

    Sale to residents

    Sale to a community land trust or co-op, targeting 1.5–2.5x invested capital.

8 · Governance and control

Who decides, and how that changes.

  • Stages 1–4: founder-led, with the Managing Member making decisions and reporting openly.
  • From Stage 5: an elected community council (one seat per 50 residents) runs operations; major decisions need 80% consent.
  • Founder veto: limited to five written core principles, not budgets or operations.
  • Key person risk: a succession and key-person plan will be written in Stage 2.
