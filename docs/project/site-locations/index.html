Site Locations

No land has been bought. This page is a desk study: it narrows the search to five regions worth visiting in Stage 2 (Design, 2027), when the final site is chosen. Two are in the United States and three are abroad, in Portugal, Spain and Costa Rica. Every option sits well above 350 ft (107 m) above sea level.

Each region is scored against the siting criteria and the energy plan: solar first, batteries, then renewable backup (biogas or bio-ethanol, never diesel), with micro-hydro and geothermal heat as site-dependent bonuses. See Resilience without diesel.

What we are looking for​

  • 100–500 acres, rural or semi-rural, for a 20–30 acre Phase 1 core plus farmland, buffers and room for Phase 2 (At a glance).
  • Higher ground: at least 350 ft (107 m) above sea level. This keeps the city clear of storm surge, sea-level rise and coastal flooding, and usually brings cooler nights.
  • Strong sun for a 2–5 MWp solar microgrid.
  • Secure water: rainfall, springs or wells, and the legal right to use them.
  • A bonus energy resource: hot water underground for direct geothermal heat, or a year-round stream for micro-hydro.
  • Codes that allow earth and timber: rammed earth, adobe, cob, compressed earth block and mass timber.
  • A clear legal path to own land for an international founding circle.
  • Affordable land, low climate risk, a welcoming community and reasonable access to a hospital and an airport.

United States options​

Option 1 · Northern New Mexico (Taos to Ojo Caliente)​

Best for: building with earth, legally, from day one.

Elevation: about 7,000 ft (2,130 m).

  • Earth-building code. New Mexico is one of the few US states with its own Earthen Building Materials Code, covering adobe, rammed earth, compressed earth block and terrón, for buildings up to two stories (NM 14.7.4.12). This fits the Beyond the box homes directly.
  • Sun. About 5.3 kWh/m²/day, among the best in the country (NASA POWER).
  • Geothermal. The Rio Grande rift drives the state's geothermal potential (NM Bureau of Geology). Ojo Caliente already uses its hot water for space heating (NM Bureau of Geology), and geothermal greenhouses make up nearly half of the state's greenhouse acreage (Earth Matters). Northern wells are warm (about 115–132°F), good for heat, not power (City of Albuquerque appendix).
  • Precedent. The Greater World Earthship community near Taos covers about 630 acres and shows the county will permit off-grid earth homes (Alta).

Watch-outs.

  • Water is scarce. Rights run through acequias and the State Engineer, and well permits can be contested (US Land Life).
  • Earthship history shows that county rules can tighten. Plan as a proper subdivision with roads, wells and wastewater approvals from the start (SAH Archipedia).
  • Taos Pueblo and Hispano land-grant communities have deep ties to this land. Any project here begins with listening, not buying.
  • Taos County residential height is capped at 27 feet without a special permit (Taos County). A 60–120 m spire would need a special use permit and may not fit here at all.

Option 2 · Klamath Basin, Southern Oregon (Klamath Falls)​

Best for: proven geothermal heat and a code friendly to cob and mass timber.

Elevation: about 4,100 ft (1,250 m).

  • Geothermal. Klamath Falls has run a city geothermal district heating system since 1981, with 200–220°F wells serving 23 buildings and melting snow from sidewalks (City of Klamath Falls, US DOE). Hundreds of homes and Oregon Tech also heat with geothermal water (OSTI). This is the strongest geothermal story of all five options.
  • Codes. Oregon's residential code includes an appendix for cob construction (Oregon BCD), and Oregon was the first state to allow tall mass-timber buildings (Oregon BCD). That suits glulam domes and earth walls.
  • Hydro. Oregon has the largest small-hydro potential of the western states studied, mostly in the Cascades (Kosnik, Energy Policy).
  • Sun. Still good at about 4.7 kWh/m²/day (NASA POWER).

Watch-outs.

  • Long-running conflict over Klamath water between farms, tribes and fish. A newcomer must not add pressure.
  • Wildfire and drought are real risks. Design for defensible space and on-site water storage.

International options​

Option 3 · Central Portugal (Viseu and São Pedro do Sul uplands)​

Best for: spa-town geothermal heat, rain, rivers and open land rules.

Elevation: about 1,500 ft (460 m) on the plateau around Viseu. Choose upland parcels, not valley floors.

  • Geothermal. São Pedro do Sul runs a geothermal district heating network on 67°C water that serves 13 hotels and 2 spas (World Geothermal Congress 2026, Portugal update). Chaves, further north, heats buildings with 77°C water, and the state keeps a register of qualified geothermal resources (DGEG). Portugal has even grown pineapples in a geothermal greenhouse (WGC 2010).
  • Water. About 39 inches of rain a year (NASA POWER), mostly in winter.
  • Earth building. Portugal has a living rammed-earth (taipa) tradition, strongest in the Alentejo, with a recent revival among newcomers (Visit Serpa). Engineers have studied how these walls behave in earthquakes (University of Minho).
  • Land law. EU and non-EU buyers can own freehold property on the same terms as Portuguese citizens (Legal 500).

Watch-outs.

  • Wildfire is the main risk. In June 2017 fires in central Portugal killed at least 64 people, most of them in Pedrógão Grande (ESA). Plan wide firebreaks, native broadleaf planting instead of eucalyptus, and on-site water storage.
  • Rustic land inside the national agricultural or ecological reserves is very hard to build on, whoever owns it (Move and Invest). Check the municipal plan before any offer.
  • Non-resident buyers of homes now pay a flat 7.5% transfer tax (Legal 500).

Option 4 · Granada highlands, Spain (Guadix and the Geopark of Granada)​

Best for: earth-sheltered living with centuries of proof, strong sun and cheap land.

Elevation: about 3,000 ft (925 m); the Guadix plateau sits above 900 m (Xataka).

  • Earth-sheltered homes. Guadix has more than 2,000 inhabited cave homes that hold a steady 18–20°C all year (Guadix tourism). Northern Granada has about 5,000, inside the UNESCO Geopark of Granada (El Periódico). This is the strongest real-world proof of the Beyond the box idea of building with the earth.
  • Geothermal. Hot springs ring the basin: Graena at 44°C and Alicún at 34°C, with estimated reservoir temperatures up to about 80–96°C (IGME). Good for direct heat and greenhouses, not power.
  • Sun. About 5.1 kWh/m²/day, the best of the European options (NASA POWER).
  • Land law. Spain does not ban foreign ownership; non-EU buyers need a military permit only in listed defence zones such as borders, islands and some coasts (UK government guidance), which do not include inland Granada.

Watch-outs.

  • Water is the hard limit: only about 13 inches of rain a year (NASA POWER). Secure rights to wells or irrigation channels first.
  • Summer days near 40°C and frosty winters. Earth sheltering helps with both.
  • Houses on rural land need confirmed planning permission from the municipality and region (UK government guidance).

Option 5 · Northern Costa Rica (Miravalles and Tenorio uplands, Guanacaste)​

Best for: a nearly all-renewable grid, abundant water and a mild climate all year.

Elevation: about 1,700 ft (530 m) on the volcanic uplands. Stay inland, far from the coast.

  • Clean grid. Costa Rica produced 97.3% of its electricity from renewables in 2025, with 262 MWe of geothermal, including the Miravalles field (163.5 MWe) in these same uplands (WGC 2026, Costa Rica update).
  • Microgrid law. Law 10086 allows renewable self-generation, with or without export to the grid and in island mode (Costa Rica legal database). That fits the Light City microgrid.
  • Water and hydro. About 57 inches of rain a year at the reference point (NASA POWER), with many year-round streams.
  • Land law. Foreigners can own titled land on the same terms as citizens; only the 200 m coastal strip is off limits (US State Department).
  • Community. A strong eco-tourism and wellness culture, and an international airport at Liberia.

Watch-outs.

  • Volcanoes and earthquakes. Earth walls need engineered reinforcement or a timber frame with earth infill.
  • Hydro depends on rain. In the 2024 El Niño drought, renewables fell to 86% of national generation (El Observador). Keep on-site storage generous.
  • Geothermal power is developed by the state, so the city would use the clean grid and heat pumps rather than drill its own wells.

Also considered​

  • San Luis Valley, Colorado (about 7,600 ft). Strong sun, cheap land and the Crestone spiritual community, with geothermal water around 200°F (Pueblo Chieftain, Town of Crestone). Moved to reserve because of very cold winters, a stressed aquifer and its remoteness.
  • Western North Carolina (about 2,100 ft). The wettest US option, with many micro-hydro sites (AZoCleantech), but Hurricane Helene in 2024 set off around 2,000 landslides (NOAA Climate.gov).
  • San Miguel de Allende, Mexico (about 6,200 ft). An adobe and arts town with strong sun (Wikipedia), and foreigners can own land inland with a Foreign Ministry permit (Library of Congress). Held back because the local aquifer has no water left to allocate (CONAGUA) and the US advises travelers to reconsider travel to Guanajuato state (US State Department).
  • Southern Idaho (Boise) (about 2,700 ft). Boise runs the largest municipal geothermal heating utility in the US, warming more than 6 million square feet with 177°F water (US DOE). A strong reserve candidate.
  • Big Island, Hawaii. Real geothermal power, but high land and shipping costs and lava risk; only upland parcels would meet the elevation rule.
  • South Florida (Homestead). The founder's home base, but it sits near sea level, so it fails the 350 ft rule. Better suited to an urban retrofit pilot than the first city.

Next steps​

  1. Founding circle input (2026). Rank the criteria: earth-building freedom, geothermal heat, water, community, or staying in the United States?
  2. Shortlist two regions (early 2027). Talk to planners, water offices, land lawyers and local and Indigenous community leaders before any land search.
  3. Site visits (2027). Walk 3–5 parcels per region above 350 ft. Check solar access, slope, soils, wells, streams, fire risk and road access.
  4. Test before buying. Commission a water-rights review, a geotechnical survey and, where geothermal is promising, a temperature-gradient well.
  5. Visionary layer, tested fairly. If the community wants to include how a place feels, use a blinded comparison of shortlisted sites so the impressions can be recorded honestly (see Open Questions).
  6. Decision gate. The site is chosen at the end of Stage 2 (Design), as set out in Development Stages.
