Similar Movements: Who Else Is Building Toward This

Status: Living research page
Last reviewed: November 2026

Light City is not the first attempt at a better kind of settlement, and it will not be the last. This page sets out the projects closest to ours, what they have actually built, and what we learn from each. Figures come from each project's own pages or from independent reporting, and every claim links to its source.

No affiliation

Light City is independent. Listing a project here does not mean it endorses Light City or that we speak for it. Where we hope to collaborate, we say so and ask first.

Visionary cities​

These projects start from a complete picture of a future city. They are our closest relatives in ambition, and they show how hard it is to move from drawings to residents.

The Venus Project​

  • What it is. A non-profit founded by Jacque Fresco and Roxanne Meadows. It proposes a resource-based economy that uses science and technology to reach "a sustainable civilization of abundance for all, without exception" (The Venus Project). Its work covers habitat design, new materials, energy and resource management, and transition strategies called Venus Evolutions.
  • What it has built. In 1980 the founders bought land in Venus, Florida, which grew to 21.5 acres. They built 3 residences and 7 other structures, including two domes that held hundreds of scale models (Past Works). They funded it from their outside jobs, without sponsors or debt (Center for Resource Management).
  • Center for Resource Management. Starting in 2017, a worldwide volunteer team held more than 300 design meetings for a self-sufficient research center shaped like Fresco's round cities. The project did not finish because of funding, and the organization describes it as a lesson in what large builds need (Past Works).
  • What we share. Systems thinking, round city forms with central domes, a city run in service of ecology, and an economy that moves away from money.
  • Where we differ. Light City starts with a small, financed pilot of 200 residents (At a Glance) and keeps money at its edge (Economy and Migration). It also welcomes a spiritual layer, clearly labeled (The Archetype). The Venus Project is strictly science-based.
  • Lesson. Volunteer design can go a long way, but construction needs land and funding secured first. We gate each stage on both (Development Stages).
  • Relationship. We reached out in November 2026 to explore sharing knowledge. The Venus Project asks groups for formal consent before they use its name and history (FAQ), and we will follow its guidance.

Arcosanti​

  • What it is. A prototype "arcology" (architecture plus ecology) by Paolo Soleri, begun in 1970 in the Arizona desert between Phoenix and Sedona (Arcosanti).
  • What it has built. More than 8,000 volunteers have helped build it since 1970. Its design called for thousands of residents, but no more than about 100 have ever lived there (Arcosanti). The target was 5,000 people. The population has varied between 50 and 150, and the most recent building was finished in 1989 (Wikipedia).
  • What we share. Compact, walkable form, cast and sculpted architecture, and the idea of a city as a living experiment.
  • Lesson. Workshop labor builds landmarks, not a town. Phase 1 of Light City is built by professionals, so homes are ready for residents on a date.

Telosa​

  • What it is. A city proposed in 2021 by entrepreneur Marc Lore for 5 million people on about 150,000 acres. Land would be owned by a community foundation under a model called "Equitism," and the first phase was to house 50,000 people on 1,500 acres by 2030 (The Spectrum). Bjarke Ingels Group designed the masterplan (Dezeen).
  • Status. No site had been chosen as of mid-2026 (AII).
  • What we share. Community ownership of land, renewable power and a 15-minute walkable layout.
  • Lesson. Even large backing does not replace a site. Site selection is on Light City's critical path (Site Locations).

Masdar City​

  • What it is. A zero-carbon, car-free city announced by Abu Dhabi in 2006. Foster + Partners designed it for 50,000 residents and 40,000 commuters on 6 km² (Economic Vision 2030).
  • What it has built. About 15,000 people in 2024 (Wikipedia). In 2025 it generated 10,797 MWh of renewable energy and cut energy use intensity by 42% against its baseline (Oil & Gas News). Critics say it drifted from ecological urbanism toward a business district (Urbanet).
  • Lesson. Efficient buildings are proven. Car-free life and new transit systems are harder. We label each part with its readiness (Technology Horizon).

Ecovillages and intentional communities​

These communities started small and have lasted for decades. They teach us the most about daily life, governance and money.

Auroville​

  • What it is. A universal township in Tamil Nadu, India, designed for up to 50,000 people from around the world (Auroville).
  • Today. 3,527 residents from 60 nations in March 2026 (Auroville census). It aims for no money changing hands among residents (Auroville A to Z).
  • Lesson. A big plan can still be worth having if growth is honest. We plan in measured steps: hamlet, village, then town.

Findhorn​

  • What it is. A spiritual community in Scotland since 1962, with roughly 300 to 500 people in the wider community. The Findhorn Foundation closed its educational programmes in September 2023 (Wikipedia).
  • Footprint. Residents' ecological footprint was 2.71 global hectares per person, about half the UK average (Findhorn footprint report).
  • Lesson. Retreat and course income can stop. We keep it off the critical path (Investors).

Twin Oaks​

  • What it is. An income-sharing community in rural Virginia, founded in 1967, with about 85 adult members and 15 children (Twin Oaks). One hour of work earns one labor credit, and credits have no cash value (labor policy).
  • Lesson. Equal hours can work for decades. This is the model behind Light hours (Economy and Migration).

Damanhur​

  • What it is. A federation of spiritual eco-communities in Piedmont, Italy, known for the underground Temples of Humankind, with its own constitution and currency (Foundation for Intentional Community). It has about 600 resident citizens living in households of 10 to 20 people (Casa Clan).
  • Its currency. The Credito is legally and fiscally regulated and equal in value to the euro (Damanhur).
  • What we share. Sacred architecture built by a community, and an inner currency alongside the national one.
  • Lesson. A spiritual community can run a legal inner currency for decades if it stays tied to the national currency and the tax rules.

Tamera​

  • What it is. A peace research and education center in Portugal. It builds "Healing Biotopes," research centers that model a nonviolent culture, including decentralized water, energy and food (Tamera).
  • What we share. A community as a living laboratory, and the link between inner change and outer design.
  • Lesson. Being clear about research, education and outreach goals helps a community explain itself to its neighbors and supporters.

Crystal Waters​

  • What it is. Founded in 1987 as the world's first intentional permaculture village, on 640 acres in Queensland. It has 83 privately owned one-acre plots and large common lands, and more than 250 residents (Crystal Waters). Its 17 dams were designed to protect water quality downstream (Crystal Waters).
  • Lesson. A legal subdivision with shared land and a co-operative can be a practical way to hold a regenerative site. We will study it during legal design.

Sieben Linden​

  • What it is. An ecovillage in Germany founded in 1997, with around 140 residents growing slowly toward a planned 300 (Global Ecovillage Network). Its residents emit about 5.0 t of CO₂ per person each year, 52% below the German average (Sieben Linden).
  • Lesson. Community-built growth is slow. Self-build at Light City is optional.

Planned towns​

These are conventional developments, not movements. They show what permits, utilities and markets allow today.

Serenbe​

  • What it is. A wellness-focused community in Georgia of about 1,500 residents and 800 homes, with 70% of its land preserved (Serenbe).
  • Lesson. Its zoning overlay took years of work with the county. Permits are a Stage 2 workstream for us.

Babcock Ranch​

  • What it is. A solar-powered town in Florida that welcomed its first residents in 2018 and had more than 15,000 residents by the end of 2025 (SWFL Business Today). Its 150 MW solar plant is owned by the utility, FPL (Babcock Ranch media kit).
  • Lesson. A utility partnership can pay for the energy system. We will test this option at every shortlisted site.

Networks​

Global Ecovillage Network​

  • What it is. A network that has connected, supported and promoted ecovillages since 1995, with communities in more than 50 countries (Global Ecovillage Network).
  • Why it matters. It is the natural home for Light City among peers, and it is a source of mentors, visits and shared tools.

What the pattern tells us​

  1. Plans usually outrun people. Arcosanti was planned for 5,000 and has had 50 to 150 people. Auroville was planned for 50,000 and has about 3,500. Masdar City was planned for 50,000 and has about 15,000. Light City's numbers are therefore stage targets, each one gated on the last (At a Glance).
  2. Volunteer energy designs; capital builds. The Venus Project's Center for Resource Management and Arcosanti both show how far volunteers can go, and where they stop.
  3. The communities that last are small, clear and patient. Twin Oaks, Findhorn, Damanhur and Crystal Waters have lasted from nearly 40 to more than 60 years with clear rules about work, money and membership.
  4. Money at the edge stays. Every long-lived community still pays taxes and outside bills in national currency. Our two-layer design follows that (Economy and Migration).
  5. Proven technology carries the load. Measured footprints at Findhorn and Sieben Linden, and the solar plant at Babcock Ranch, rely on proven tools, not frontier ones.

How we hope to relate​

  • Learn openly. Visit, read and cite before we design.
  • Share back. Our library is open under CC BY-SA 4.0, so any of these projects may use it.
  • Collaborate with consent. We contact each project before naming any partnership, and we describe others' work in their own words.
